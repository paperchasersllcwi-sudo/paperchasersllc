Coming Home From Prison in Wisconsin: What Families and Returning Citizens Need to Know About Supervision

By Michael Winius, Paper Chasers LLC
Disclaimer: This article is provided for educational and informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. Paper Chasers LLC is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice or legal representation. Individual supervision rules and court orders vary. Always read and follow your own rules, and ask your agent when something is unclear.

Introduction

If you are sitting in a Wisconsin prison waiting for your release date, this article is for you. It is also for the people waiting for you: your parents, your partner, your kids, your brother or sister, or the friend letting you sleep in the spare bedroom. Everyone is excited that you are coming home. But coming home does not mean you are free to do whatever you want.

If you are released to probation, parole, or extended supervision, you are leaving the institution, but you are still under the authority of the Wisconsin Department of Corrections (DOC). I have made that transition from a Wisconsin prison back into the community myself, more than once. Here is what I want both the person coming home and their family to understand.

"He's Out Now." Not Exactly.

One of the biggest misconceptions families have is, "He's done his time. Why can't he just live his life?" That is not how community supervision works. DOC's Division of Community Corrections supervises people on probation, parole, and extended supervision, and supervision carries rules set by the court, by DOC, and by the supervising agent. Those rules can reach everyday life: where you live, where you go, who you are around, what you are around, travel, treatment, police contact, and more.

Wisconsin's Standard Rules of Community Supervision warn that failing to meet supervision expectations can result in progressive sanctions, up to and including revocation. Something does not have to be a new felony to create a supervision problem.

Families: don't say, "Come on, you're out. Your agent isn't going to know." Don't put the person you love in the position of choosing between making you happy and following their rules. Instead ask, "What are your rules? What can I do to help you follow them?" That is support.

Prepare the House Before Release

If someone is coming home to live with you, don't wait until they are standing in your living room to think about their supervision. DOC begins release planning well before release, including the proposed residence, employment, treatment, and transportation. Families should be planning too. Look around the residence:

  • Are there firearms in the house?
  • Alcohol or illegal drugs?
  • Prescription medications that belong to other people?
  • Anything else that could conflict with this person's specific rules?
  • Who comes to the house, who hangs out there, and who stays overnight?

The rules govern the supervised person, not automatically everyone else in the home. But the household may need to identify potential conflicts before they become problems. Talk with the homeowner or leaseholder about what can reasonably be addressed. And if you don't know whether something violates a supervision condition, don't guess. Ask the supervising agent. An uncomfortable question beforehand is better than explaining an avoidable problem afterward.

Know Your Rules, Not Somebody Else's

People on supervision talk. "My agent lets me do that." "Nobody checks that." "I've been doing it for six months." None of that tells you what your rules are. An agent may impose additional rules related to the individual or the offense, and a sentencing court may impose its own conditions. Two people sitting next to each other can have different restrictions.

  • Read your rules.
  • Keep a copy.
  • Ask questions, and get clarification when something doesn't make sense.

Curfews and Overnight Stays

Time can look like a minor issue until it isn't. Picture your first Christmas home in years. Everyone wants you to stay "just one more hour." But if you have a time you are required to be home, your family's emotional argument doesn't change your supervision requirements. Plan for the worst-case scenario, not the best case: how long it normally takes to get home, and how long it could take. And being allowed to visit somewhere does not automatically mean you are allowed to sleep there. Know the rule before you make the plan.

When Something Goes Wrong: Communicate and Document

Sometimes you do everything right and life still happens. It's December in Wisconsin, the snow is coming down, traffic is crawling, and you realize you are not going to make it home on time. Don't make the situation more dangerous trying to beat the clock, but don't disappear either. When I was on supervision and faced a situation like that, I created a record: call the agent; if there's no answer, leave a voicemail; send an email explaining what is happening.

Sending an email does not magically excuse a violation. It means you are not hiding what happened. There is a big difference between saying, "Here's what happened, here's when I called, here's the email I sent," and having your agent discover the problem later and ask why you never made contact.

That's why I preach this constantly: document everything. Keep emails, important text messages, relevant receipts, appointment information, and notes of important conversations. If your agent tells you to do something important and you are unsure about it, ask for clarification. You are building a history of your own behavior, and six months later, a record made at the time is far more persuasive than memory.

Don't Hide Police Contact

Wisconsin's Standard Rules of Community Supervision require a supervised person to report any arrest or police contact to their agent within 72 hours. Notice what that says: not just convictions, and not just arrests. Police contact. Don't assume, "I wasn't arrested, so I don't have to say anything." Read your rules and follow them. Families, don't encourage someone to conceal something from their agent because you are afraid they will get in trouble. That can make a bad situation considerably worse.

What Happens When There's a Violation?

People hear "violation" and immediately think "revocation" and "back to prison." That is not automatically the result of every violation. Wisconsin's supervision rules describe progressive sanctions that can extend up to revocation, and the response depends on the circumstances and the procedures that apply. That's why your behavior before, during, and after a problem matters. Did you communicate? Did you take responsibility? Did you document what happened? Did you follow directions afterward? Or did you disappear?

For a detailed look at DOC holds, Alternatives to Revocation, and the revocation hearing process, read Violated Probation in Wisconsin? What Happens Next.

For Families: What Support Really Looks Like

The person coming home is going to need you. But support doesn't mean removing every obstacle, and it definitely doesn't mean encouraging them to ignore supervision. Sometimes support sounds like:

  • "What time do you have to be home?"
  • "Do you need a ride?"
  • "Is it okay for you to be around this?"
  • "Do you need to call your agent before we make these plans?"

Sometimes the most loving thing you can do is tell everyone else, "No, he's got to go." Don't embarrass someone for following their rules. Remember what they are trying to accomplish: they are trying to stay home.

Start Planning Before the Gate Opens

Don't wait until release morning. DOC describes institutional release planning as beginning months before release and coordinating with the assigned probation and parole agent, covering residence, employment, education or treatment, transportation, clothing, vital documents, and other reentry needs. Use that time:

Talk to your social worker
Ask questions about your release plan and find out who your agent will be when that information is available.
Lock down the basics
Work on your residence, transportation, and employment, and get your vital documents together.
Talk with your family
Share your rules with the people you'll live with so the house is ready before you arrive.

The Effort Pays Dividends

Every appointment you keep, every time you follow through, every hard "No, I can't do that," every time you communicate instead of hiding, and every day you go to work and keep your residence stable, you are putting something in the bank. The payoff isn't money. It is your life: getting through supervision, staying in the community, rebuilding relationships and trust, building a career, being there for your kids, and eventually reaching the day when the Department of Corrections isn't part of your daily life anymore.

The prison gate opening isn't the finish line. It's the starting line. Know your rules, communicate, document everything, and stay free.
