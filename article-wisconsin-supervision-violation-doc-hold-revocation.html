Violated Probation in Wisconsin? What Happens Next: DOC Holds, ATRs, and Revocation

By Michael Winius, Paper Chasers LLC
Disclaimer: This article is provided for educational and informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. Paper Chasers LLC is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice or legal representation. Every person's case, sentence, supervision rules, and circumstances are different. If the Department of Corrections is pursuing revocation, speak with a qualified attorney.

Watch Episode 3 of The People's Paralegal, where I walk through my own supervision violation, from a DOC hold to a 60-day sanction. Open on YouTube

Introduction

If you are on probation, parole, or extended supervision in Wisconsin and something goes wrong, such as drinking, missing curfew, police contact, or a new arrest, the questions come fast. Will my agent put me in jail? Am I getting revoked? Am I going back to prison? This article explains how the process generally works: DOC holds, investigations, sanctions, Alternatives to Revocation (ATRs), revocation hearings, and what can happen afterward.

I am not writing this from the outside. I have been on supervision, I have violated supervision, and I have sat in jail waiting to learn what the Department of Corrections (DOC) would do with me. I share that story in the video above.

Report Police Contact. It Is a Rule.

Wisconsin's Standard Rules of Community Supervision require people under supervision to report any arrest or police contact to their agent within 72 hours. When I was arrested for a DUI while on supervision, I told my agent everything. He told me to report to the jail, and I did.

Self-reporting does not guarantee a particular outcome, and it does not erase the underlying conduct. But running from your agent or lying about what happened can turn one problem into several.

What Is a DOC Hold?

A DOC hold is temporary custody, usually in a county jail, while DOC investigates a possible supervision violation or decides how to respond. DOC describes holds as being placed and lifted at the discretion of the Division of Community Corrections.

This is something families often miss. A person can be sitting in jail on a supervision hold even though there is no new criminal case on Wisconsin CCAP. A DOC hold and a new criminal prosecution are two different things. If you are searching CCAP and asking, "There are no new charges, so why won't they let him out?", a hold is a likely reason. (See Understanding Wisconsin CCAP for what CCAP can and cannot show.)

During a hold, the agent investigates what happened and decides what response is appropriate. In my case, I sat in jail about a week before I learned the outcome.

Does Every Violation Mean Revocation?

No. Wisconsin DOC's rules contemplate progressive responses to violations, up to and including revocation. DOC's client materials state that alternatives to revocation must be considered when revocation is being contemplated. The response can depend on what happened, how serious it was, the person's overall adjustment on supervision, risk, and other circumstances.

What Is an ATR (Alternative to Revocation)?

An Alternative to Revocation is an agreement in which DOC does not pursue revocation if the supervised person successfully completes specified requirements. Depending on the situation, DOC materials describe alternatives that can include:

  • A period of jail time as a sanction
  • Electronic monitoring
  • Alcohol monitoring, such as continuous or scheduled breath testing
  • Amended or additional supervision conditions
  • Community programming or treatment
  • Residential or inpatient services
  • Institution-based programming

My outcome was an ATR with a 60-day jail sanction. After that came treatment, 90 days of portable alcohol monitoring, and no-alcohol and no-drug requirements. The consequences did not end when the jail door opened.

When DOC Decides to Pursue Revocation

Revocation may be pursued for new-law violations or serious rule violations. The decision is not simply the agent acting alone; DOC materials describe supervisory and regional review of a decision to seek revocation.

An agent recommending revocation is not the final word. In Wisconsin, adult probation, parole, and extended-supervision revocation hearings are handled by the Division of Hearings and Appeals (DHA), an agency separate from DOC. Unless the person waives the hearing, revocation requires an administrative hearing.

The Revocation Hearing

If DOC proceeds, the supervised person receives notice of the alleged violations and is advised of hearing rights, including the right to a hearing before an administrative law judge (ALJ) and the right to be represented by an attorney. At the hearing, the supervised person, their attorney, and the agent can present evidence, including witness testimony, documents, and audio or video recordings. Witnesses can be questioned, and testimony is recorded.

The ALJ decides two separate questions:

1. Did the violation happen?
Does the evidence establish the alleged supervision violation?
2. Is revocation appropriate?
Even if a violation occurred, is revoking supervision the right response?
3. Written decision
DHA ordinarily issues a written decision within 10 working days after the hearing.

"But They Didn't Charge Me With a Crime"

A common misconception is that if the district attorney does not file charges, or a criminal case is dismissed, DOC cannot do anything. Do not make that assumption. Supervision proceedings and criminal prosecutions are separate processes. A supervision violation can be based on conduct that breaks a supervision rule, which reaches more broadly than a new criminal conviction. Keep these two questions separate:

  • Am I being criminally prosecuted?
  • Is DOC alleging that I violated my supervision?

What Happens If Supervision Is Revoked?

It depends on the sentence. Probation, parole, and extended supervision are not interchangeable. Revocation can result in a return to court for sentencing (for example, in some probation cases) or incarceration in jail or prison. What happened to someone else, such as "my cousin got revoked and this is what happened," does not tell you what will happen in your case. Know what type of supervision you are on, know your sentence, and if DOC is pursuing revocation, talk to an attorney who can review your specific situation.

For Families: What You Can Do

If someone you love calls and says, "I'm sitting in jail on a probation hold":

  • Don't panic, and don't assume.
  • Find out where they are being held.
  • Find out whether there are new criminal charges, a supervision violation allegation, or both.
  • Preserve legitimate documentation that may matter, such as messages, receipts, employment records, or location records.
  • Do not play investigator, and never contact alleged victims or witnesses to try to change their story.
  • If revocation is being pursued, help your loved one get appropriate legal assistance.
  • Expect some waiting. Answers are not always immediate.

Document Everything

If an alleged violation concerns where you were, whether you attended treatment, whether you were working, or when something happened, records made at the time can matter. That does not mean creating evidence after the fact. It means building the habit of keeping legitimate records while you are on supervision: emails, appointment records, employment records, receipts, relevant messages, instructions from your agent, and treatment attendance. If you face a revocation proceeding, let your attorney decide what matters and how it should be used.

The Question I Wish I'd Asked

Before my DUI, my thinking was simple: I'm not going to get caught. Then I was caught, and I spent the next several months paying for it: the hold, 60 days in jail, treatment, alcohol monitoring, and additional rules. Before you risk your freedom, don't ask whether you'll get caught. Ask what happens if you do, and whether it is really worth it.

If a violation has already happened, your job is different: don't make it worse, understand what is being alleged, understand your rights, preserve legitimate documentation, follow lawful instructions, and seriously consider legal representation if revocation is on the table.
