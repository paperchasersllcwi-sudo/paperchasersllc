Wisconsin Commutations Explained: Understanding Sentence Reduction Through Executive Clemency in Wisconsin

By Michael Winius, Paper Chasers LLC
Disclaimer: This article is provided for educational and informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. Paper Chasers LLC is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice or legal representation.

Introduction

Most people are familiar with pardons. Far fewer understand commutations. Although both are forms of executive clemency, they serve different purposes. A pardon generally benefits an individual who has completed a sentence. A commutation applies to a person who is still serving a sentence and seeks a reduction or modification of that sentence.

Constitutional Authority

The authority to grant commutations originates directly from the Wisconsin Constitution. Article V, Section 6 provides that the Governor may grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons after conviction. The authority belongs exclusively to the Governor and remains entirely discretionary.

What Is a Commutation?

A commutation is a reduction or modification of an existing criminal sentence. Unlike a pardon, a commutation does not forgive a conviction. Unlike an appeal, it does not require proof of legal error. It is an exercise of executive discretion and mercy. Examples may include:

  • Reducing a term of confinement
  • Advancing a release date
  • Reducing a life sentence
  • Modifying portions of a sentence
  • Reducing supervision obligations

Commutations Versus Pardons

A pardon generally applies after sentence completion and may restore certain rights. A commutation applies during an active sentence and directly affects the sentence itself.

Historical Background

For approximately twenty-five years Wisconsin did not maintain an active statewide commutation process. In 2026, Governor Tony Evers restored the process and established the Wisconsin Commutation Advisory Board through Executive Orders 287 and 288.

Why Was the Process Restored?

Public statements from the Governor's Office cited rehabilitation, humanitarian concerns, review of lengthy sentences, and public safety considerations as reasons for restoring the process.

The Wisconsin Commutation Advisory Board

The Board reviews applications, conducts hearings, considers victim input, evaluates applicants, and makes recommendations to the Governor. The Board does not grant commutations. Final authority remains with the Governor.

Who May Apply?

Eligibility standards are established through executive policy and application materials. Potential considerations include:

  • Nature of offense
  • Time served
  • Institutional conduct
  • Rehabilitation efforts
  • Public safety concerns
  • Victim notification requirements

Eligibility does not guarantee relief.

What Factors Appear Relevant?

Common considerations include:

  • Rehabilitation
  • Educational achievement
  • Vocational training
  • Treatment completion
  • Positive institutional conduct
  • Public safety
  • Victim input
  • Humanitarian concerns

What a Commutation Does Not Do

A commutation does not:

  • Vacate a conviction
  • Expunge a record
  • Declare innocence
  • Erase criminal history

The conviction remains valid while the sentence is modified.

Why Commutations Matter

Commutations occupy a unique role within Wisconsin's criminal justice system. Courts focus upon legality and appeals focus upon legal error. Commutations focus upon mercy, rehabilitation, and executive discretion.

Common Misconceptions

A commutation does not erase a conviction. A commutation is not the same as a pardon. The Governor is not required to follow Board recommendations. Rehabilitation alone does not guarantee relief.

Conclusion

The restoration of Wisconsin's commutation process represents one of the most significant developments in executive clemency policy in recent decades. Although commutations remain rare and highly discretionary, they provide a meaningful constitutional avenue through which qualified individuals may seek review of an ongoing sentence.
