How to Build a Strong Wisconsin Commutation Application: Documentation, Rehabilitation, and Presentation

By Michael Winius, Paper Chasers LLC
Disclaimer: This article is provided for educational and informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. The Wisconsin commutation process is discretionary and no applicant is entitled to relief. Paper Chasers LLC is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice or legal representation.

Introduction

The restoration of Wisconsin's commutation process created a pathway through which certain incarcerated individuals may seek executive review of an ongoing sentence. The strongest applications generally focus on rehabilitation, accountability, institutional success, and readiness for reintegration into society.

Understanding the Goal

A commutation application is not an appeal, a sentence modification motion, or an opportunity to relitigate guilt or innocence. It is a request for executive mercy. The central question is often: Why should the Governor reduce this sentence today?

Section One: Institutional Record

Applicants should gather records concerning:

  • Conduct reports
  • Disciplinary history
  • Program participation
  • Institutional employment
  • Educational achievements

Section Two: Educational Accomplishments

Helpful documentation includes:

  • GED completion
  • College coursework
  • Vocational training
  • Apprenticeship programs
  • Technical certifications

Section Three: Treatment and Rehabilitation

Applicants should document participation in:

  • AODA treatment
  • Mental health treatment
  • Anger management
  • Cognitive intervention programs
  • Other rehabilitative services

Section Four: Employment Preparation

Examples of supporting materials include:

  • Vocational certificates
  • Employment training records
  • Trade certifications
  • Employer support letters
  • Evidence of employability

Section Five: Family and Community Support

Applicants should identify:

  • Family support
  • Housing resources
  • Religious organizations
  • Mentors
  • Community assistance programs

Section Six: Release Plan

A strong release plan should address:

  • Housing
  • Employment
  • Transportation
  • Treatment and counseling
  • Community support

The more specific the plan, the stronger the application generally becomes.

Section Seven: Character Reference Letters

Potential authors include:

  • Family members
  • Employers
  • Clergy
  • Educators
  • Community leaders
  • Mentors

Strong letters describe personal knowledge of the applicant, observed growth, and reasons supporting commutation.

Section Eight: Acceptance of Responsibility

Applicants should demonstrate:

  • Understanding of the offense
  • Recognition of harm caused
  • Lessons learned
  • Personal growth

Attempts to shift blame often undermine credibility.

Section Nine: Demonstrating Rehabilitation

The application should answer:

  • How has the applicant changed?
  • What evidence supports that change?
  • Why is the change likely to continue?

Section Ten: Public Safety Considerations

Applicants should address:

  • Institutional conduct
  • Treatment participation
  • Educational achievements
  • Community support
  • Employment opportunities
  • Age and maturity

The goal is to demonstrate that release would be consistent with public safety.

Common Mistakes

  • Failing to provide documentation
  • Submitting a weak release plan
  • Focusing exclusively on the sentence rather than rehabilitation
  • Minimizing responsibility
  • Providing vague statements unsupported by evidence

Practical Lesson

The strongest applications generally demonstrate that the applicant has changed, is prepared for release, has support, and can successfully reintegrate into society.

Conclusion

Although commutations remain discretionary and relatively rare, applicants can strengthen their presentation through documentation, accountability, rehabilitation, institutional success, public safety considerations, and a detailed release plan.
