Liberal Construction of Pro Se Pleadings: How Wisconsin and Federal Courts Read Filings by Self-Represented People

Research Library › Court Records & Procedure
By Michael Winius, Litigation Paralegal, Paper Chasers LLC · November 2026
Disclaimer: This article is provided for educational and informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. Paper Chasers LLC is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice or legal representation. It explains how courts have treated filings by self-represented people; it does not predict how any court will treat a particular filing. For advice about a specific case, speak with a licensed Wisconsin attorney.

The Short Answer

If you have ever filed something in court without a lawyer, you may have heard that judges must "liberally construe" pro se pleadings. That is true, in both Wisconsin's state courts and the federal courts. But it is often misunderstood.

Liberal construction means a court reads a self-represented person's filing for its substance, not its form. Courts hold it to "less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted by lawyers," look past a wrong title, and usually allow at least one chance to fix a deficient complaint. The leniency is about interpretation, not exemption. A self-represented person must still state facts that support a claim, meet deadlines, follow procedural rules, and develop their own arguments. Courts will not act as anyone's lawyer.

The federal anchor is Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519, 520–21 (1972) (per curiam), restated in Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (per curiam). The Wisconsin anchors are State ex rel. Terry v. Traeger, 60 Wis. 2d 490, 496, 211 N.W.2d 4 (1973), and bin-Rilla v. Israel, 113 Wis. 2d 514, 520–21, 335 N.W.2d 384 (1983), with the limits set by Waushara County v. Graf, 166 Wis. 2d 442, 452, 480 N.W.2d 16 (1992).

Part I. The Federal Foundation

The U.S. Supreme Court built the doctrine in a series of short opinions, then fenced it in with a second line of cases holding that leniency does not excuse procedural default.

The statutes and rules

  • Right to proceed pro se. "In all courts of the United States the parties may plead and conduct their own cases personally or by counsel . . . ." 28 U.S.C. § 1654.
  • Construction of pleadings. "Pleadings must be construed so as to do justice." Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(e). Rule 8(a)(2) requires only "a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief."
  • Screening. Federal courts must screen prisoner complaints against government defendants, 28 U.S.C. § 1915A, and in forma pauperis complaints, 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2)(B), and dismiss those that are frivolous, malicious, or fail to state a claim. Liberal construction is applied at screening, which is where many pro se cases are won or lost.

The core cases: what liberal construction requires

  1. Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519, 520–21 (1972) (per curiam). A prisoner's pro se civil rights complaint was dismissed. The Court reversed, holding that the allegations of a pro se complaint, "however inartfully pleaded," are held "to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted by lawyers." Haines is the source of the modern doctrine.
  2. Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 106 (1976). The Court reaffirmed Haines, but also held that the prisoner's medical-care allegations, read liberally, still showed at most negligence, not deliberate indifference. Liberal reading does not create facts that are not there.
  3. Hughes v. Rowe, 449 U.S. 5, 9–10 (1980) (per curiam). The Court applied Haines to reverse the dismissal of a prisoner's segregation claim, and limited fee awards against pro se plaintiffs to truly frivolous suits.
  4. Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (per curiam). A prisoner alleged that officials cut off his hepatitis C treatment. The lower court dismissed his harm allegations as "conclusory." The Supreme Court summarily reversed: "A document filed pro se is 'to be liberally construed,' and 'a pro se complaint, however inartfully pleaded, must be held to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted by lawyers.'" Id. (quoting Estelle, 429 U.S. at 106). Today this is the standard citation for the rule.
  5. Castro v. United States, 540 U.S. 375, 381–83 (2003). Federal courts may ignore the label a pro se litigant puts on a motion and recharacterize it "to avoid an unnecessary dismissal, to avoid inappropriately stringent application of formal labeling requirements, or to create a better correspondence between the substance of a pro se motion's claim and its underlying legal basis." Id. at 381–82 (citations omitted). But before converting a filing into a first 28 U.S.C. § 2255 motion, the court must notify the litigant, warn that later § 2255 motions will face "second or successive" limits, and allow the litigant to withdraw or amend. Id. at 383.

The limits the Supreme Court has drawn

  • Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 834 n.46 (1975). The right of self-representation is not "a license not to comply with relevant rules of procedural and substantive law."
  • McNeil v. United States, 508 U.S. 106, 113 (1993). A pro se plaintiff sued under the Federal Tort Claims Act before finishing the required agency claim process. The dismissal was affirmed. Pro se pleadings are liberally construed, and some rules bend for incarcerated litigants, see Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266, 276 (1988) (prison mailbox rule), but the Court has "never suggested that procedural rules in ordinary civil litigation should be interpreted so as to excuse mistakes by those who proceed without counsel."
  • Pliler v. Ford, 542 U.S. 225, 231 (2004). "District judges have no obligation to act as counsel or paralegal to pro se litigants." Requiring judges to advise pro se parties on strategy would undermine their role as impartial decisionmakers. Id.
  • Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007), and Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The "plausibility" standard applies to every civil complaint. Erickson softens how a pro se complaint is read; it does not lower the requirement that the facts alleged, taken as true, make a claim plausible. The older "no set of facts" phrasing that Haines relied on was retired by Twombly, 550 U.S. at 562–63.

Part II. The Seventh Circuit and Wisconsin's Federal Courts

Wisconsin's two federal districts, the Eastern District (Milwaukee and Green Bay) and the Western District (Madison), follow Seventh Circuit precedent. In practice, the circuit puts the leniency to work in four places: reading the complaint, granting leave to amend, giving summary-judgment notice, and recruiting counsel. It draws an equally firm line on appellate briefing and procedural compliance.

Reading the complaint

The court construes a pro se complaint liberally and accepts its factual allegations as true. Luevano v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 722 F.3d 1014, 1027 (7th Cir. 2013); Terry v. Spencer, 888 F.3d 890, 893 (7th Cir. 2018). In Luevano, a store greeter's pro se Title VII complaint, written on the court's fill-in form, was dismissed. The Seventh Circuit reversed: read liberally, her allegations of supervisor-tolerated harassment and retaliation were enough. 722 F.3d at 1027–29. In Tate v. SCR Medical Transportation, 809 F.3d 343, 346 (7th Cir. 2015), the court held that a pro se discrimination complaint met the "undemanding" pleading standard and should not have been dismissed at screening.

Leave to amend before dismissal

Before dismissing at screening, a district court should give a litigant, "especially a pro se litigant," a chance to amend "unless it is certain from the face of the complaint that any amendment would be futile or otherwise unwarranted." Tate, 809 F.3d at 346 (quoting Barry Aviation Inc. v. Land O'Lakes Mun. Airport Comm'n, 377 F.3d 682, 687 (7th Cir. 2004)); see Luevano, 722 F.3d at 1022–25; Runnion ex rel. Runnion v. Girl Scouts of Greater Chi. & Nw. Ind., 786 F.3d 510, 519–20 (7th Cir. 2015). But the court need not tell a litigant what to amend: "even pro se litigants are masters of their own complaints." Myles v. United States, 416 F.3d 551, 552 (7th Cir. 2005).

Summary-judgment notice

A pro se litigant facing a summary-judgment motion must be told, in plain English, that the other side's sworn facts will be accepted as true unless the litigant contradicts them with their own affidavits or other evidence, with the text of Rule 56 attached. Lewis v. Faulkner, 689 F.2d 100, 102–03 (7th Cir. 1982) (prisoners); Timms v. Frank, 953 F.2d 281, 285 (7th Cir. 1992) (all pro se litigants). The rule exists because answering a motion with counter-affidavits is "contrary to lay intuition." Timms, 953 F.2d at 285. If the moving party leaves the notice out, the court must give it, Lewis, 689 F.2d at 103, but a missing notice is harmless if the litigant would have lost anyway, Timms, 953 F.2d at 287. The Eastern District of Wisconsin builds the requirement into its local rules. E.D. Wis. Civ. L.R. 56(a).

Recruitment of counsel

There is no right to appointed counsel in federal civil cases, but a court may ask a volunteer lawyer to help under 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(1). The test asks whether the litigant made a reasonable attempt to get a lawyer and whether, "given the difficulty of the case," the litigant "appear[s] competent to litigate it himself." Pruitt v. Mote, 503 F.3d 647, 654–55 (7th Cir. 2007) (en banc). The inquiry is "particularized to the person and case." Id. at 656.

Where the Seventh Circuit draws the line

  • "[P]ro se litigants are not excused from compliance with procedural rules." Pearle Vision, Inc. v. Romm, 541 F.3d 751, 758 (7th Cir. 2008); see Members v. Paige, 140 F.3d 699, 702 (7th Cir. 1998).
  • On appeal, the court construes pro se filings liberally, but "must be able to discern cogent arguments in any appellate brief, even one from a pro se litigant." Anderson v. Hardman, 241 F.3d 544, 545 (7th Cir. 2001). It "cannot fill the void by crafting arguments and performing the necessary legal research." Id. Briefs that only narrate grievances are routinely dismissed under Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(8).

Part III. Wisconsin's Constitutional, Statutory, and Rule Basis

In Wisconsin, liberal construction is not just case law. It rests on the state constitution, the civil procedure code, the small claims chapter, and the Code of Judicial Conduct.

Part IV. Wisconsin Case Law

Wisconsin's doctrine grew up in prisoner cases and then merged with the state's general rule that all pleadings are construed liberally. Its two working tools are reading for facts rather than legal theories, and relabeling a mistitled filing.

The foundation: Terry and bin-Rilla

  1. State ex rel. Terry v. Traeger, 60 Wis. 2d 490, 496–99, 211 N.W.2d 4 (1973). A prisoner wrote a letter to a judge challenging a prison regulation. The Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized that confinement, prison rules, and the fact that many prisoners are "unlettered" and most are indigent make legal help hard to get. It held that courts "must follow a liberal policy in judging the sufficiency of pro se complaints." Id. at 496. The letter stated a claim for declaratory relief, id. at 497, and a court may treat such a letter as a habeas petition, order a response, and appoint counsel, id. at 498–99.
  2. bin-Rilla v. Israel, 113 Wis. 2d 514, 335 N.W.2d 384 (1983). A prisoner's habeas petition was hard to understand. The supreme court held that pro se prisoner complaints, "whether offered in petition or any other form, including letters to judges, must be construed liberally to determine if the complaint states any facts giving rise to a cause of action." Id. at 520. Courts "look to the facts pleaded, not to the label given the papers filed." Id. at 520–21. And the key working rule: "Neither a trial nor an appellate court should deny a prisoner's pleading based on its label rather than on its allegations. If necessary the court should relabel the prisoner's pleading and proceed from there." Id. at 521. The court found a possible claim of denial of access to the courts (confiscated legal papers), held that habeas was the wrong vehicle, and transferred the matter to the circuit court rather than dismissing it. Id. at 522–24.

The rule extended

  • Lewis v. Sullivan, 188 Wis. 2d 157, 164–65, 524 N.W.2d 630 (1994). Wisconsin has long construed pleadings liberally to do substantial justice between the parties. Reading a prisoner's complaint about seized property liberally, the court found a due process claim and claims for declaratory, injunctive, and replevin-type relief.
  • State ex rel. Adell v. Smith, 2001 WI App 168, ¶¶ 6–7, 247 Wis. 2d 260, 633 N.W.2d 231. A Wisconsin pleading needs only "fair notice," a principle that applies especially to pro se prisoner pleadings. A complaint should be dismissed only if it is "quite clear that under no conditions can a plaintiff recover."
  • State ex rel. Nichols v. Litscher, 2001 WI 119, ¶ 32, 247 Wis. 2d 1013, 635 N.W.2d 292. The 30-day deadline for a pro se prisoner's petition for review is tolled when the prisoner delivers a correctly addressed petition to the proper prison authorities for mailing. The rule is now in Wis. Stat. § (Rule) 809.80(3). The court warned that the tolling rule will not excuse failing to pay fees, address the petition properly, or otherwise comply with filing requirements.

The Wisconsin limits

  • Waushara County v. Graf, 166 Wis. 2d 442, 452, 480 N.W.2d 16 (1992). The leading limiting case, cited in nearly every pro se appeal. "Pro se appellants must satisfy all procedural requirements, unless those requirements are waived by the court." Id. Self-representation is not "a license not to comply with relevant rules of procedural and substantive law." Id. (quoting Faretta, 422 U.S. at 834 n.46). Wisconsin courts routinely cite Graf for the point that, while some leniency may be allowed, neither a trial court nor a reviewing court "has a duty to walk pro se litigants through the procedural requirements or to point them to the proper substantive law." Graf also noted that special leniency on the waiver of rights had been extended only to pro se prisoners.
  • State v. Pettit, 171 Wis. 2d 627, 646–47, 492 N.W.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1992). Arguments that are undeveloped or unsupported by legal authority will not be considered; a court "cannot serve as both advocate and judge." Id. at 647. See also Indus. Risk Insurers v. Am. Eng'g Testing, Inc., 2009 WI App 62, ¶ 25, 318 Wis. 2d 148, 769 N.W.2d 82 ("[W]e will not abandon our neutrality to develop arguments.").
  • Holz v. Busy Bees Contracting, Inc., 223 Wis. 2d 598, 608, 589 N.W.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1998). Like lawyers, pro se litigants must make a reasonable investigation of the facts and the law before filing an appeal.

Part V. What Liberal Construction Does, and Does Not, Do

Both court systems draw the same line: the court will interpret generously, but it will not advocate.

The representation limit

Liberal construction protects only a person representing himself or herself. A nonlawyer cannot represent another person or a business.

  • Federal court. A corporation, partnership, or association "may appear in the federal courts only through licensed counsel." Rowland v. Cal. Men's Colony, Unit II Men's Advisory Council, 506 U.S. 194, 201–02 (1993); United States v. Hagerman, 545 F.3d 579, 581 (7th Cir. 2008).
  • Wisconsin court. A notice of appeal signed by a nonlawyer on behalf of a corporation is fundamentally defective, and filing it is the unauthorized practice of law under Wis. Stat. § 757.30. Jadair Inc. v. U.S. Fire Ins. Co., 209 Wis. 2d 187, 202, 213, 562 N.W.2d 401 (1997). The statutory exception lets a full-time authorized employee appear for an entity in small claims. Wis. Stat. § 799.06(2); Holz, 223 Wis. 2d at 603–06.

Part VI. Case Examples: The Doctrine in Practice

The pattern is consistent. Self-represented people tend to succeed when the facts are there and only the form is wrong. They lose when the facts, the argument, or a required procedural step is missing.

A worked example

Suppose a tenant files a paper in a Wisconsin circuit court titled "Motion to Stop Eviction" after a judgment, saying she never received the summons. Under bin-Rilla, the court should look past the title and ask what relief the facts could support. Her allegations might be read as a request for relief from judgment under Wis. Stat. § 806.07. Liberal construction gets her filing considered under the right rule. It does not relieve her of that rule's own requirements, such as acting within the required time and showing a recognized ground. See Graf, 166 Wis. 2d at 452. (This is an illustration only; the result in any real case depends on its record.)

Part VII. Lessons From the Case Law

These points describe how courts have applied the doctrine. They are general education, not advice for any particular case.

  1. Facts carry the filing, not legal labels. The successful cases above turned on concrete facts: who did what, when, and what harm followed. A filing heavy on legal conclusions and light on facts gets little help from liberal construction after Iqbal.
  2. A wrong title is usually survivable; a missing step is not. Courts relabel motions and petitions (bin-Rilla, Castro). They do not forgive missed deadlines, skipped exhaustion, or defective service (McNeil, Graf).
  3. Amendment matters. Federal courts in the Seventh Circuit generally allow at least one amendment before dismissal (Luevano, Tate), and Wisconsin's § 802.09 says leave should be freely given when justice requires. The court will not say what to fix (Myles).
  4. Summary judgment turns on evidence, not argument. The Lewis/Timms notice exists because people without lawyers often do not realize they must respond with affidavits or declarations (28 U.S.C. § 1746) and specific factual responses.
  5. On appeal, the leniency narrows sharply. Briefs must follow Wis. Stat. § (Rule) 809.19 or Fed. R. App. P. 28 and make developed, cited arguments (Pettit, Anderson).
  6. Self-representation is personal. A nonlawyer may not sign or argue for a spouse, a relative, or a business (Rowland, Jadair, Wis. Stat. § 757.30), except an authorized full-time employee in small claims (§ 799.06(2)).
  7. Judges may help with process, not strategy. In Wisconsin, SCR 60.04(1)(hm) allows plain-language explanations and neutral questions. Neither court system lets the judge act as counsel (Pliler).
