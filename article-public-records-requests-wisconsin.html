Public Records Requests in Wisconsin: Understanding Wisconsin's Open Records Law

Part 1: What the Open Records Law Is

Wisconsin’s Open Records Law, which the statutes call the public records law, is the set of rules that lets members of the public see the records their government keeps. If a state agency, county, city, village, town, school district, or police department creates or keeps a record, the starting presumption is that you may look at it, unless a specific law or a strong public reason says otherwise.

The law took much of its present form in 1981. It is found in Wis. Stat. §§ 19.31–19.39. A separate but related law, the Open Meetings Law (Wis. Stat. §§ 19.81–19.98), governs when government bodies must meet in public. The two are often called Wisconsin’s “sunshine laws.”

Why the Legislature Wrote It

The Legislature explained its reasons in the statute itself. Section 19.31 begins by recognizing that “a representative government is dependent upon an informed electorate,” and declares that all persons are entitled to “the greatest possible information regarding the affairs of government and the official acts of those officers and employees who represent them.” Wis. Stat. § 19.31.

The same section tells courts and records custodians how to read the rest of the law: with “a presumption of complete public access,” because “[t]he denial of public access generally is contrary to the public interest.” Id. In practice, that means openness is the rule and withholding is the exception, and the government carries the burden of justifying the exception.

The Law in One Paragraph

Anyone may ask. You usually do not have to say who you are or why you want the record. The government must respond as soon as practicable and without delay. It may charge only certain actual costs. If it says no, it must explain why, and a written denial must tell you how to seek review. If a record is wrongfully withheld, a court can order it released. Parts 2 and 3 explain each of these points.

Part 2: What the Law Says

This part walks through the statutes in order. Section numbers are given so readers can look up the current text at docs.legis.wisconsin.gov.

§ 19.32 — Key Definitions

“Authority.” The law applies to “authorities,” a broad term covering state and local offices, elected officials, agencies, boards, commissions, councils, departments, school districts, and certain governmental and quasi-governmental corporations, among others. Wis. Stat. § 19.32(1). Each authority must have a legal custodian responsible for responding to requests. Wis. Stat. § 19.33.

“Record.” A record is any material on which written, drawn, printed, spoken, visual, or electromagnetic information or electronically generated or stored data is recorded or preserved, regardless of physical form, that has been created or is being kept by an authority. Wis. Stat. § 19.32(2). That includes paper files, emails, text messages, photographs, audio and video recordings, databases, maps, and more.

Some things are not records, including:

  • Drafts, notes, preliminary computations, and similar materials prepared for the originator’s personal use;
  • Materials that are purely the personal property of the custodian and have no relation to the office;
  • Materials whose access is limited by copyright, patent, or bequest; and
  • Published materials that are available for sale or available for inspection at a public library.

Id. The government is also not required to create a new record to answer a request. The law gives access to records that exist; it is not a way to make officials answer questions. See Journal Times, discussed in Part 3.

“Requester.” A requester is generally any person who asks to inspect or copy a record. There is one important exception: a committed or incarcerated person is a “requester” only when asking for records that contain specific references to that person or to his or her minor children (for whom physical placement has not been denied), and only if the record is otherwise accessible to that person by law. Wis. Stat. § 19.32(3). In other words, a person in prison or jail generally may request records about himself or herself, but not records about other subjects.

§ 19.35(1) — The Right to Inspect

The core rule is simple: except as otherwise provided by law, any requester has a right to inspect any record. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(1)(a). Individuals also have an additional right to inspect records containing personally identifiable information about themselves, subject to exceptions. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(1)(am).

No identity or reason required. A request generally may not be refused because the requester will not give his or her name or will not explain why the record is wanted. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(1)(i). There are narrow exceptions, such as records kept at a private residence or situations where security reasons or federal law require identification. Id.

Oral or written. A request may be made orally. However, a request must be in writing before an enforcement action may be started under § 19.37. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(1)(h). That is the practical reason to put a request in writing.

Reasonably specific. A request must reasonably describe the record or information requested. A request with no reasonable limit as to subject matter or length of time is not a sufficient request. Id.

§ 19.35(3) — Fees

An authority may charge only for certain actual costs:

According to the Wisconsin Department of Justice, the time spent reviewing and redacting records is not part of “location” and may not be charged to the requester. Wis. Dep’t of Just., Wisconsin Public Records Law Compliance Guide (June 2025). There is no automatic right to free copies for people who cannot afford them. George v. Record Custodian, 169 Wis. 2d 573, 485 N.W.2d 460 (Ct. App. 1992). A requester may still ask for a waiver under § 19.35(3)(e).

§ 19.35(4) — Timing and Denials

Timing. The authority must, “as soon as practicable and without delay,” either fill the request or notify the requester of its decision to deny it in whole or in part and the reasons. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(4)(a). The statute sets no fixed number of days. The Department of Justice has stated that, generally, 10 working days is a reasonable time to respond to a simple request. Wis. Dep’t of Just., Compliance Guide, supra. Larger or more complex requests may reasonably take longer.

Denials. If an oral request is denied orally, the requester may demand a written statement of the reasons within 5 business days. A written request that is denied in whole or in part must be answered with a written statement of the reasons. Every written denial must tell the requester that the decision is subject to review by mandamus under § 19.37(1) or upon application to the attorney general or a district attorney. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(4)(b).

Partial release. When part of a record is confidential, the authority generally must release the rest with the confidential parts removed (redacted), rather than withhold the whole record. Wis. Stat. § 19.36(6).

§ 19.35(5) — No Destroying Requested Records

Once a request is received, the authority may not destroy the record until the request is granted or until at least 60 days after the request is denied. If the requester is a committed or incarcerated person, that period is at least 90 days after denial. If a lawsuit is filed, the record must be kept until the court case is over. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(5).

§ 19.36 — Exceptions and the Balancing Test

Records may be withheld for three general kinds of reasons:

  • Statutory exceptions. Another state or federal law makes the record confidential (for example, many juvenile, medical, and certain law enforcement records). Wis. Stat. § 19.36(1)–(2).
  • Common-law exceptions. Long-recognized court rules, such as the attorney-client privilege.
  • The public-interest balancing test. Even when no specific exception applies, the custodian must weigh the public interest in disclosure against the public interest in nondisclosure. Only if the harm to the public interest from release outweighs the strong public interest in openness may the record be withheld.

The balancing test is done case by case, based on the specific record and circumstances. The identity of the requester and the reason for the request are generally not part of the test. The cases in Part 3 show the balancing test in action.

§ 19.37 — Enforcement

If an authority withholds or delays a record after a written request, the requester has two options, and may use either or both:

  • File an action for mandamus, a court order directing the authority to release the record. Wis. Stat. § 19.37(1)(a).
  • Ask, in writing, the district attorney of the county where the record is found, or the attorney general, to bring the mandamus action. Wis. Stat. § 19.37(1)(b). They may, but are not required to, do so.

What a requester can recover. If the requester prevails in whole or in substantial part, the court shall award reasonable attorney fees, damages of not less than $100, and other actual costs. Wis. Stat. § 19.37(2)(a). If the court finds the authority arbitrarily and capriciously denied or delayed a response or charged excessive fees, it may award punitive damages, Wis. Stat. § 19.37(3), and the authority or custodian may be required to forfeit up to $1,000, Wis. Stat. § 19.37(4).

Important limits:

  • A person representing himself or herself is not entitled to attorney fees. State ex rel. Young v. Shaw, 165 Wis. 2d 276, 477 N.W.2d 340 (Ct. App. 1991).
  • A committed or incarcerated requester is not entitled to the $100 minimum damages, although a court may still award damages. Wis. Stat. § 19.37(2)(a).
  • A committed or incarcerated person must file a mandamus action no later than 90 days after the request is denied. Wis. Stat. § 19.37(1m).
  • An inmate must also exhaust administrative remedies before filing. Moore v. Stahowiak, 212 Wis. 2d 744, 569 N.W.2d 711 (Ct. App. 1997); see Wis. Stat. § 801.02(7).
  • Under Friends of Frame Park (Part 3), a requester generally “prevails” only by obtaining a court order, not because the government released records after a lawsuit was filed.

Anyone may also ask the attorney general for advice about how the law applies. Wis. Stat. § 19.39. The Department of Justice’s Office of Open Government publishes the Compliance Guide and answers public questions.

A Related Statute: Body Cameras, § 165.87

Because body-camera video is one of the most-requested law enforcement records, the Legislature added specific rules in 2020. 2019 Wis. Act 108 (creating Wis. Stat. § 165.87).

  • Agencies that use body cameras must have a written policy and, if they have a website, post that policy publicly. Wis. Stat. § 165.87(1).
  • Body-camera data generally must be kept for at least 120 days after recording, and longer when it records certain events, such as a death, an injury, a use of force, or an arrest, or when a proceeding is pending. Wis. Stat. § 165.87(2).
  • Body-camera data may not be destroyed while a public records request for it is pending. Wis. Stat. § 165.87(2)(e).
  • Body-camera data is open to inspection and copying under § 19.35(1), with added privacy protections for victims of sensitive crimes, minors, and people recorded in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Wis. Stat. § 165.87(3).

Part 3: The Cases That Define It

Statutes set the rules, and court decisions show how the rules apply to real situations. Each case below is summarized for educational purposes, followed by the main point it teaches. Readers should consult the full opinions for complete reasoning.

1. Linzmeyer v. Forcey, 2002 WI 84 — A Neenah Police Report

What happened. The Neenah Police Department investigated allegations that Armand Linzmeyer, a math teacher and volleyball coach at Neenah High School, had acted inappropriately toward female students. He was never arrested or prosecuted. The Appleton Post-Crescent and the parents of students requested the police report. The Neenah city attorney planned to release it, and Linzmeyer sued to stop the release, arguing it would harm his reputation and privacy.

What the court decided. The Wisconsin Supreme Court affirmed the release. Applying the balancing test, it held that the public’s interest in knowing that police investigate properly, and in the conduct of a teacher serving the public, outweighed the teacher’s interest in his reputation. The presumption of openness was not overcome.

The lesson. A record does not become secret just because it is embarrassing or because no charges were filed. The court also recognized, however, that police reports can raise special concerns (such as protecting witnesses and investigations) that must be weighed in each case.

2. Hempel v. City of Baraboo, 2005 WI 120 — When the Balance Tips the Other Way

What happened. A Baraboo police officer, Hal Hempel, was accused of sexual harassment by a fellow officer, and the department conducted an internal investigation. Hempel requested the investigation records, including witness statements. The city denied the request.

What the court decided. The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the denial. It reaffirmed that Wisconsin policy favors the broadest practical access and that access may be denied only in exceptional cases. But it held that this was such a case: the city’s policy of keeping witnesses in harassment investigations confidential weighed against release, because complainants and witnesses would be less willing to come forward if their statements could be made public.

The lesson. The balancing test is real. It usually favors release, but a custodian may withhold records when it can identify a specific, concrete public interest that outweighs openness in that particular situation.

3. WIREdata, Inc. v. Village of Sussex, 2008 WI 69 — Format and Contractors

What happened. A company requested property assessment records from three municipalities in an “electronic/digital” format. The records were kept in a database maintained by private contractor assessors. The municipalities provided PDF files, but the company wanted access to the underlying database.

What the court decided. The Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the PDFs satisfied the request because they were in an electronic format, and that the independent contractor assessors were not themselves “authorities” that a request could be sent to. The municipalities, not their contractors, remained responsible for providing access to the records.

The lesson. A government body cannot avoid the law by handing its records to a contractor, but a requester is entitled to the record in a reasonable form, not necessarily the exact format he or she would prefer. Be specific about format in the request.

4. Schill v. Wisconsin Rapids School District, 2010 WI 86 — Personal Emails

What happened. A resident requested all emails sent and received by five teachers on their school district email accounts over a period of several weeks. The district allowed occasional personal use of school email. The teachers sued to stop release of their purely personal emails.

What the court decided. In a 5–2 decision, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the contents of purely personal emails, which have no connection to government business, are not subject to disclosure under the public records law, even though they were stored on a government system.

The lesson. What makes something a public record is its connection to government business, not where it is stored. Emails about official business remain public records, and custodians must review requested emails to separate the two.

5. Journal Times v. City of Racine Board of Police & Fire Commissioners, 2015 WI 56 — Records, Not Answers

What happened. Racine’s Police and Fire Commission held a closed session while selecting a new police chief. The Journal Times newspaper requested records of the motions made and the individual votes taken. No written minutes existed at the time of the request. The newspaper sued.

What the court decided. The Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the newspaper did not prevail in substantial part and was not entitled to fees and damages. The public records law gives access to records that exist; it does not require an authority to create a record that does not exist or simply to answer questions. The court also held that a claimed violation of the Open Meetings Law cannot be pursued under the public records law.

The lesson. Phrase a request as a request for records (“all minutes, notes, recordings, and emails reflecting...”), not as a question (“How did each member vote?”). If you believe a meeting itself was improperly closed, that is an Open Meetings Law issue.

6. Friends of Frame Park, U.A. v. City of Waukesha, 2022 WI 57 — Who “Prevails”

What happened. A community group requested records about the City of Waukesha’s plan to build a baseball stadium in Frame Park. The city withheld a draft contract until after a Common Council meeting. The group sued, and the city released the contract shortly afterward. The group then sought its attorney fees.

What the court decided. The Wisconsin Supreme Court held that to “prevail in whole or in substantial part” under § 19.37(2)(a), a requester must obtain a judicially sanctioned change in the parties’ legal relationship, generally a court order. Because the city released the record on its own, and the court concluded the record had initially been properly withheld, the group was not entitled to fees. This replaced the older approach, under which a requester could recover fees by showing the lawsuit caused the release.

The lesson. Filing a lawsuit may lead to release of records, but it does not guarantee recovery of costs. A bill to reverse this decision (2025 S.B. 194) passed the Senate in May 2025 but was not adopted by the Assembly before the session ended in March 2026, so as of this update Frame Park remains the governing rule.

Part 4: Putting It Together

Elements of a Clear Request

Based on the statutes and cases above, a clear written request typically:

  • Is addressed to the records custodian of the agency that actually keeps the record;
  • States that it is a request under Wisconsin’s public records law, Wis. Stat. §§ 19.31–19.39;
  • Asks for records, not answers to questions;
  • Reasonably describes the records, with names, dates, locations, and case or incident numbers where known;
  • Sets a reasonable time period;
  • States the preferred format (paper, PDF, native electronic file, video file);
  • Asks to be told in advance if fees will exceed a set amount; and
  • Keeps a copy and notes the date sent.

Example Request Format

The following is a general example of how a request is commonly organized. It is provided for educational purposes only and is not a form approved by any agency or a substitute for legal advice.

[Date]

Records Custodian
[Agency name]
[Address or email]

Re: Public Records Request

Under Wisconsin’s public records law, Wis. Stat. §§ 19.31–19.39, I request copies of the following records:

[Describe the records, for example: “The incident report, any supplemental reports, and any body-camera and squad video for Incident No. ______, dated ______, at ______.”]

I request the records in electronic form if available. If fees will exceed $____, please notify me before proceeding. If any part of this request is denied, please provide the reasons in writing as required by Wis. Stat. § 19.35(4)(b) and release any portions that are not confidential.

Thank you,
[Name and contact information]

CCAP Is Not the Same as a Records Request

Wisconsin Circuit Court Access (CCAP) shows court case information, such as filings, hearing dates, and outcomes. It does not contain police reports, body-camera video, jail records, agency emails, or most other government records. Those come from the agency that keeps them, through a public records request. For more on what CCAP shows, see Understanding Wisconsin CCAP: What the Public Can and Cannot See.

Common Misconceptions

  • “I need a lawyer to ask for records.” Generally not. Anyone may make a request.
  • “I have to explain why I want it.” Generally not. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(1)(i).
  • “Everything the government has must be released.” No. Statutory exceptions, common-law exceptions, and the balancing test can limit access.
  • “They have to answer my questions.” No. The law covers existing records. Journal Times, 2015 WI 56.
  • “If they release it after I sue, they pay my costs.” Not necessarily. Frame Park, 2022 WI 57.
  • “Copies are free if I can’t afford them.” Not automatically, though a waiver may be requested. Wis. Stat. § 19.35(3)(e).

Conclusion

Wisconsin’s Open Records Law starts from a simple idea written into the statute itself: people who govern themselves need to know what their government is doing. The statutes make access the default, limit what can be charged, require written reasons for denials, and give courts power to order release. The cases show where the edges are: embarrassment alone is not enough to withhold a record (Linzmeyer), concrete confidentiality interests sometimes are (Hempel), personal material is not a record (Schill), format and contractors have limits (WIREdata), the law covers records rather than questions (Journal Times), and recovering costs requires a court win (Frame Park). Understanding those rules helps members of the public use the law effectively and respectfully.
